# 1.11 Evaluation Metrics

## Introduction

Chapters 1.4 and 1.10 explained *how to split data* so that performance estimates are honest. This chapter explains *what to measure*. A metric turns the model's predictions and the true outcomes into a number, and the choice of metric determines what "good" means. A model that looks excellent under one metric can be useless under another: a classifier that always predicts "no fraud" is 99.9% accurate on data where 0.1% of transactions are fraudulent [1], [2].

We cover the confusion matrix and the metrics derived from it for classification, threshold-free metrics such as ROC-AUC and PR-AUC, and the standard regression metrics. We also discuss how class imbalance and the cost of different errors should drive the choice.

## Learning Objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

- Build and interpret a confusion matrix.
- Compute accuracy, precision, recall, specificity, F1, and the $F_\beta$ family.
- Explain ROC curves and AUC, and when precision-recall curves are more informative.
- Choose between micro, macro, and weighted averaging in multiclass problems.
- Compute and interpret MSE, RMSE, MAE, and $R^2$ for regression.
- Select metrics that match the real-world costs of errors.

## Fundamental Concepts

### The Confusion Matrix

For a binary problem with a "positive" class of interest, every prediction falls in one of four cells [2], [3]:

|  | Predicted positive | Predicted negative |
|--|--------------------|--------------------|
| **Actually positive** | True positive (TP) | False negative (FN) |
| **Actually negative** | False positive (FP) | True negative (TN) |

A false positive is a false alarm (a healthy patient flagged as ill). A false negative is a miss (an ill patient not flagged). These two errors usually have different costs.

### Classification Metrics

| Metric | Question it answers | Weakness |
|--------|---------------------|----------|
| Accuracy | What fraction of all predictions is correct? | Misleading with imbalanced classes |
| Precision | Of the cases flagged positive, how many truly are? | Ignores missed positives |
| Recall (sensitivity) | Of the true positives, how many were found? | Ignores false alarms |
| Specificity | Of the true negatives, how many were correctly cleared? | Ignores missed positives |
| F1 score | Is there a balance of precision and recall? | Ignores true negatives; treats both errors equally |

When missing a positive is costly (disease screening), prioritize recall. When false alarms are costly (flagging a legitimate transaction and blocking a customer), prioritize precision [2], [3].

### Thresholds, ROC, and Precision-Recall Curves

Many classifiers output a score or probability, and a **decision threshold** converts it into a class. Lowering the threshold finds more positives (higher recall) but raises false alarms (lower precision). Threshold-free summaries evaluate the model across all thresholds:

- The **ROC curve** plots the true-positive rate (recall) against the false-positive rate. Its area, **ROC-AUC**, equals the probability that a randomly chosen positive example gets a higher score than a randomly chosen negative one. A value of 0.5 means random ranking and 1.0 means perfect ranking [3].
- The **precision-recall (PR) curve** plots precision against recall, and its summary is average precision (AP). With highly imbalanced data, ROC curves can look deceptively good because the false-positive rate is diluted by the large number of negatives; PR curves are more sensitive to the performance on the minority class [4].

### Probabilistic Metrics

**Log loss** (cross-entropy) evaluates the predicted probabilities themselves, penalizing confident wrong predictions heavily. It also serves as the training loss for logistic regression. A model can rank examples well (high AUC) yet give poorly calibrated probabilities (poor log loss) [1].

### Multiclass Averaging

For more than two classes, per-class metrics must be combined [2]:

| Average | How | Best when |
|---------|-----|-----------|
| Macro | Compute the metric per class, then take the unweighted mean | All classes matter equally, including rare ones |
| Weighted | Per-class mean weighted by class size | You want to reflect class frequencies |
| Micro | Pool all TP, FP, FN across classes, then compute the metric | Overall example-level performance |

### Regression Metrics

| Metric | Formula idea | Properties |
|--------|--------------|------------|
| MSE | Mean of squared errors | Penalizes large errors heavily; units are squared |
| RMSE | Square root of MSE | Same units as the target; still sensitive to outliers |
| MAE | Mean of absolute errors | Robust to outliers; easy to interpret |
| $R^2$ | Fraction of variance explained | Unitless; can be negative on new data |

## Mathematical Foundations

From the confusion-matrix counts:

$$
\text{Accuracy}=\frac{TP+TN}{TP+TN+FP+FN},\qquad
\text{Precision}=\frac{TP}{TP+FP},\qquad
\text{Recall}=\frac{TP}{TP+FN}
$$

$$
\text{Specificity}=\frac{TN}{TN+FP},\qquad
\text{FPR}=\frac{FP}{FP+TN}=1-\text{Specificity}
$$

where TP, TN, FP, and FN are the counts defined in the confusion matrix above.

The **F1 score** is the harmonic mean of precision $P$ and recall $R$, and the **$F_\beta$ score** generalizes it:

$$
F_1=\frac{2PR}{P+R},\qquad F_\beta=(1+\beta^2)\frac{PR}{\beta^2P+R}
$$

where $\beta>1$ weights recall more than precision and $\beta<1$ weights precision more. The harmonic mean is low if either component is low, so one cannot compensate for the other.

**ROC-AUC** can be written as a ranking probability:

$$
\text{AUC}=\Pr\bigl(s(\mathbf{x}^{+})>s(\mathbf{x}^{-})\bigr)+\tfrac12\Pr\bigl(s(\mathbf{x}^{+})=s(\mathbf{x}^{-})\bigr)
$$

where $s(\cdot)$ is the model's score, $\mathbf{x}^{+}$ is a random positive example and $\mathbf{x}^{-}$ a random negative one [3].

**Binary log loss** for predicted probability $\hat{p}_i$ of the positive class and true label $y_i\in\{0,1\}$:

$$
\text{LogLoss}=-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Bigl[y_i\ln\hat{p}_i+(1-y_i)\ln(1-\hat{p}_i)\Bigr]
$$

**Regression metrics**, with true values $y_i$, predictions $\hat{y}_i$, and mean target $\bar{y}$:

$$
\text{MSE}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i-\hat{y}_i)^2,\quad
\text{RMSE}=\sqrt{\text{MSE}},\quad
\text{MAE}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lvert y_i-\hat{y}_i\rvert
$$

$$
R^2=1-\frac{\sum_{i}(y_i-\hat{y}_i)^2}{\sum_{i}(y_i-\bar{y})^2}
$$

$R^2$ compares the model against the trivial model that always predicts the mean $\bar{y}$: it equals 1 for perfect predictions, 0 when the model is no better than the mean, and is negative when it is worse [1].

**A worked imbalance example.** Suppose 1% of 10,000 cases are positive (100 positives) and a model finds 60 of them while raising 140 false alarms. Then $TP=60$, $FN=40$, $FP=140$, $TN=9{,}760$. Accuracy is $9{,}820/10{,}000=98.2\%$ (which sounds excellent), but precision is $60/200=0.30$, recall is $0.60$, and $F_1=2(0.30)(0.60)/(0.90)=0.40$. A model that predicts "negative" for everyone gets 99% accuracy with zero recall.

## Practical Applications

The experiment below trains a classifier on a synthetic dataset with about 5% positives and reports several metrics. Notice how a high accuracy coexists with modest recall on the rare class, and how ROC-AUC and average precision tell different stories.

```python
from sklearn.datasets import make_classification
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.metrics import (confusion_matrix, classification_report,
                             roc_auc_score, average_precision_score,
                             accuracy_score, log_loss)

X, y = make_classification(n_samples=5000, n_features=10, n_informative=4,
                           weights=[0.95, 0.05], class_sep=1.5, flip_y=0.01,
                           random_state=0)
X_tr, X_te, y_tr, y_te = train_test_split(
    X, y, test_size=0.3, stratify=y, random_state=0)

clf = LogisticRegression(max_iter=2000).fit(X_tr, y_tr)
proba = clf.predict_proba(X_te)[:, 1]
pred = (proba >= 0.5).astype(int)

print("positive rate:", round(y_te.mean(), 3))
print("accuracy:", round(accuracy_score(y_te, pred), 3))
print("ROC-AUC:", round(roc_auc_score(y_te, proba), 3))
print("average precision:", round(average_precision_score(y_te, proba), 3))
print("log loss:", round(log_loss(y_te, proba), 3))
print(confusion_matrix(y_te, pred))
print(classification_report(y_te, pred, digits=3))
```

Typical choices: recall-oriented metrics for disease screening, precision-oriented ones for spam filtering that must not delete legitimate mail, PR-AUC for rare-event detection, RMSE when large errors are especially costly, and MAE when robustness to outliers matters.

## Common Mistakes

- **Relying on accuracy for imbalanced data.** A trivial majority-class model can score very high.
- **Optimizing the wrong metric.** The metric should reflect the real cost of false positives and false negatives.
- **Tuning the threshold on the test set.** Choose it on validation data.
- **Reporting AUC alone with rare positives.** Also report precision-recall information [4].
- **Averaging incorrectly in multiclass problems.** Macro, micro, and weighted averages answer different questions; state which one you used.
- **Using $R^2$ as the only regression metric.** It hides the scale of errors and can mislead on non-linear or out-of-sample data.
- **Confusing probability quality with ranking quality.** High AUC does not imply well-calibrated probabilities.

## Chapter Summary

- The confusion matrix (TP, FP, FN, TN) is the basis of most classification metrics.
- Accuracy suits balanced data; precision, recall, and $F_\beta$ suit imbalanced data and unequal error costs.
- ROC-AUC summarizes ranking quality across thresholds; precision-recall curves are more informative when positives are rare.
- Log loss assesses the quality of predicted probabilities.
- For regression, MSE and RMSE punish large errors, MAE is more robust, and $R^2$ measures variance explained.
- Choose metrics according to the costs of errors in the real application, and report several.
